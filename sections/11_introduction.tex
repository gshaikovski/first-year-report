\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Research into language models have adopted techniques from human reasoning to solve complex problems, such as Chain-of-Thought~(CoT), which breaks a solution into simpler chunks and solves the problem step by step~\citep{nyeShowYourWork2021,weiChainofThoughtPromptingElicits2023,kojimaLargeLanguageModels2022}.
While this improves performance on simpler tasks, it is unfit for complex problems, as direct step-by-step solution does not capture the process of reasoning that was used to arrive to the solution.
Hence, \citet{xiangSystem2Reasoning2025} argue, and I echo the argument in this proposal, that we should teach models an intermediate reasoning process that was used to arrive to the direct solution, if they are to achieve general intelligence and self-improvement.

The question then becomes which intermediate reasoning process the models should learn in order to attain high performance on complex tasks at the lowest cost, which translates into a question of the right data and the training method.
Cognitive Science holds that people solve complex problems by a process similar to heuristic search.
In the theory of \citet{newell1972human}, a person represents an unfamiliar problem as a space of states and operators, explores and evaluates new states, and abandons unpromising directions.
Behavioural modelling supports this account~\citep{vanopheusdenExpertiseIncreasesPlanning2023} and shows that the skill is general, as people adapt their search strategy to the structure of the problem~\citep{callawayRationalUseCognitive2022}.
The process proceeds sequentially until a solution is found, or the attempt is abandoned~\citep{CognitiveSearchEvolution2012,fuInterfacingMindEnvironment2015a}.

A naive approach to adopt such reasoning in language models bootstraps their fine-tuning stage from a small dataset of recorded human reasoning traces~\citep{deepseek-aiDeepSeekR1IncentivizingReasoning2025,guhaOpenThoughtsDataRecipes2025,zhangSurveyReinforcementLearning2025}, which appears to lead to suboptimal, inconsistent, and inefficient reasoning in the models~\citep{gandhiFourHabitsHighly2025,chenNOTThinkThat2025,wangThoughtsAreAll,pipisWaitWaitWait2026,arcuschinChainofThoughtReasoningWild}.
This suggests that most recorded examples of human reasoning may not provide enough structure and information about the actual process.
An alternative, advocated by \citet{xiangSystem2Reasoning2025}, is to teach the models to imitate a systematic, structured, and sound process, such as algorithmic search, and execute it in the models' context~\citep{lehnertBetterPlanningTransformers2024,gandhiStreamSearchSoS2024,kimASTROTeachingLanguage2025}.
A structured process can be predicted, which means it can be validated, monitored, and adjusted, it can be tuned with process reward feedback, which addresses the credit-assignment problem~\citep{uesatoSolvingMathWord2022,lightmanLetsVerifyStep2023}, and it can be improved systematically.

Despite the apparent benefits, the literature on the utility of systematic search imitation is scarce and ambiguous, with small-scale studies reaching conflicting conclusions~\citep{lehnertBetterPlanningTransformers2024,suDualformerControllableFast2025,saparovTransformersStruggleLearn2025,selAlgorithmThoughtsEnhancing2024,schultzMasteringBoardGames2025,kangLinTreeImprovingLLM2026,gandhiFourHabitsHighly2025,gandhiStreamSearchSoS2024,caiExtrapolationAssociationLength2025}, including that in-context search can be inefficient or unnecessary for generalisation on some search problems~\citep{qinBacktrackNotBacktrack2025,valmeekamSemanticsUnreasonableEffectiveness2025,katzThoughtSearchPlanning2024}.
Therefore, I ask the following question:

\begin{tcolorbox}[title=Thesis Question, colback=ThesisPurple!5, colframe=ThesisPurple, fonttitle=\bfseries, fontupper=\itshape]
Should language models learn to imitate a structured and systematic search process to solve complex problems?
\end{tcolorbox}

I address this question by identifying and closing three gaps in the literature: which properties of a problem make search imitation more efficient than learning from solutions directly (Gap~1), which ingredients of the search trace make it more efficient and whether they can be supplied without a symbolic trace (Gap~2), and whether the learned search can generalise to problems larger than those seen in training (Gap~3).
Closing them will advance the field past proof-of-concept studies on small datasets that investigate narrow questions, and contribute to the community a rigorous and actionable analysis of this reasoning strategy.

The rest of this proposal is organised as follows.
Chapter~\ref{ch:background} reviews state-space search, intermediate computation in language models, and the literature that combines them, and states the three gaps.
Chapter~\ref{ch:progress_report} presents the first study of this project, which addresses parts of the first two gaps and provides baselines for the third.
Chapter~\ref{ch:proposal} states the research questions, the plan for each, and the success criteria and risks.
Chapter~\ref{ch:workplan} presents the timetable for completing the thesis.
