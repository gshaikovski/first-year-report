\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:progress_motivation}

Section~\ref{sec:bg_search_lm_incontext} shows that the current literature in unclear about the utility of in-context search.
% SA supervision outperforms SO when both are trained on the same fixed dataset~\citep{lehnertBetterPlanningTransformers2024,gandhiStreamSearchSoS2024}; yet with more data SO models can reach the performance of SA models~\citep{lehnertBetterPlanningTransformers2024}, SO models with test-time scaling can outperform SA models at test-time compute parity~\citep{qinBacktrackNotBacktrack2025}, and traces semantically unrelated to the problem can perform as well as genuine traces~\citep{valmeekamSemanticsUnreasonableEffectiveness2025}.
On one hand, SA supervision improves sample efficiency~\citep{lehnertBetterPlanningTransformers2024,gandhiStreamSearchSoS2024}; on the other, learning a search algorithm may be unnecessary, as the traces can be corrupted~\citep{suDualformerControllableFast2025}, replaced~\citep{valmeekamSemanticsUnreasonableEffectiveness2025}, or outperformed with more data~\citep{lehnertBetterPlanningTransformers2024} or more test-time compute~\citep{qinBacktrackNotBacktrack2025}.
However, as Section~\ref{sec:bg_gap_efficiency} argues, none of these studies compare two approaches on matched compute, and all fix the dataset size below the size at which two approaches generalise, thus providing inconclusive evidence.
Therefore, in this chapter we ask: \emph{how do the relative performance and the compute efficiency of SA and SO models compare across search problems as the available data and model parameters increase?}

We use two search problems with different structure, representation, and heuristics.
In maze navigation (Figure~\ref{fig:problem_maze}), the state graph is explicit: the grid is listed in the prompt, and the edges are implicit pairs of adjacent cells; search traces are generated by the A\textsuperscript{*} algorithm, following~\citet{lehnertBetterPlanningTransformers2024}.
In Countdown (Figure~\ref{fig:problem_countdown}), the graph is implicit: the prompt gives only a list of integers and a target, and every intermediate state must be computed by applying arithmetic operations; search traces are generated by depth-first search, following~\citet{gandhiStreamSearchSoS2024}.
We use both a version of the game from~\citet{gandhiStreamSearchSoS2024} and add our own version called `modular Countdown', in which arithmetic is performed modulo a fixed integer.
Increasing puzzle complexity increases the size of the state-space graph, while changing arithmetic changes the transition function, which in turn impacts the size and branching factor of the graph as well as the correlation of magnitude-based heuristics with the solution; both interventions are designed to test the impact of the graph size and heuristics on the two reasoning approaches while the encoding, the trace grammar, the model, and the training procedure stay fixed.

We train GPT-2 transformers~\citep{radfordLanguageModelsAre} from scratch on SA or SO data, training on increasing fractions of the full dataset to account for data-scaling effects.
% each run completes its own learning-rate schedule, and we run each setup five times with different seeds.
Models are compared at matched training compute measured in FLOPs and at matched test-time compute following~\citet{qinBacktrackNotBacktrack2025}.

\paragraph{Maze navigation is search-optional.}
% TODO: Figure: final_maze_vs_flops (solution error vs training FLOPs, SA vs SO, three depths).
Our results presented in Figure~\ref{fig:final_maze_error} show that SO models can reach the same near-perfect accuracy as SA models with less total training compute, require marginally more data with smaller models, and close the remaining sample-efficiency gap with larger models while preserving the compute advantage of SO models.
Search traces therefore have limited effect on accuracy, but a substantial negative effect on training and test-time compute efficiency on this problem.

\paragraph{Countdown is search-efficient.}
% TODO: Figure: final_countdown_error and countdown_pass_at_k_flops.
Our experiments suggest that SA models with optimal DFS traces learn the task most efficiently (Figures~\ref{fig:final_countdown_sos_20m_error} and ~\ref{fig:final_countdown_error}).
% On SoS Countdown, SO models generalise only after seeing 120x more data than SA models, which takes 5x more training FLOPs.
% On modular Countdown, SO models with 6x more parameters fail to generalise in our experiments even after training on 200x more examples seen and 140x more FLOPs than the most compute-efficient SA model.
% At test-time compute parity, the best SO model with sampling and a verifier reaches 75\% accuracy, compared with the near-perfect accuracy for a single SA rollout, and the test-time scaling curves of the 59M- and 344M-parameter SO models overlap, so model size provides little benefit in this regime.
Compared to maze navigation, this result inverts the argument for search trace utility, which motivates our hypothesis that relative efficiency of SA depends on the problem structure.
This result feeds directly into the work that will address Gap~1~(Section~\ref{sec:proposal_rq1}).

Another result in this experiment highlights how the trace itself affects learning efficiency.
The original SA traces from~\citet{gandhiStreamSearchSoS2024} were derived from suboptimal search strategies designed to fail $43\%$ of the time.
In our experiments, we find they underperform SO as sample size increases and saturate above the $5\%$ solution error threshold, hence never achieving ID generalisation.
This provides evidence that even in the search-efficient regime, a suboptimal trace can limit learning.
The same traces were also used to train SA models, which was the compared to SO models at test-time compute parity by~\citet{qinBacktrackNotBacktrack2025}, which may explain why SO models outperformed SA models there.
This result highlights the importance of considering the trace structure when analysing learning efficiency, contradicts some conclusions in prior work discussed in Section~\ref{sec:bg_gap_trace}, and will be considered later in Section~\ref{sec:proposal_rq2} where we investigate the ingredients that enable efficient learning with search traces.

\paragraph{Two types of search tasks.}
Our experiments suggest there exist at least two types of problems with very different scaling behaviour and relative efficiency and performance of SA supervision compared to SO models.
This observation is formalised in the following definition, which we use throughout Chapter~\ref{ch:proposal}.
\begin{tcolorbox}[title=Definition (Search task types), colback=ThesisGrey!5, colframe=ThesisGrey, fonttitle=\bfseries]
For a supervision mode $m \in \{\text{SA}, \text{SO}\}$ and a model size $N$, let $C_m(\epsilon; N)$ be the least training compute, and $D_m(\epsilon; N)$ the smallest number of training samples, with which a size-$N$ model trained under mode $m$ reaches held-out solution error $\epsilon$, each minimised separately over remaining hyperparameters.
We consider target errors $\epsilon$ between the trivial baseline and the irreducible error of the task, and assume both modes can reach any such $\epsilon$ given sufficient (finite) data and model parameters.
In this context, we say a learning problem is $\epsilon$-\emph{search-optional} if, for every model size $N_\epsilon$ at which SA reaches error $\epsilon$, there is a model size $N$ with $C_\text{SO}(\epsilon; N) \le C_\text{SA}(\epsilon; N_\epsilon)$ and $D_\text{SO}(\epsilon; N) \le D_\text{SA}(\epsilon; N_\epsilon)$. Intuitively, this implies that an SO model can achieve the same error as any SA model within the same training compute and data budget.
In contrast, we say a learning problem is $\epsilon$-\emph{search-efficient} if there is a model size $N_\epsilon$ such that every model size $N$ satisfies $C_\text{SO}(\epsilon; N) > C_\text{SA}(\epsilon; N_\epsilon)$ and $D_\text{SO}(\epsilon; N) > D_\text{SA}(\epsilon; N_\epsilon)$. Intuitively, this implies that there exists at least one SA model that reaches error $\epsilon$ with less training compute and data than every SO model with the same error.
\end{tcolorbox}
% We hypothesise that the regime is determined by the problem and its structure.
Our experiments provide evidence that maze navigation is search-optional, while Countdown is search-efficient.
Crucially, SA models outperform SO models at small dataset sizes in both regimes, so a comparison at a single dataset size cannot identify the regime; this is why the prior comparisons described in Section~\ref{sec:bg_search_lm_incontext} disagree.

Following~\citet{valmeekamSemanticsUnreasonableEffectiveness2025}, we experiment with training SA models on unrelated traces and show that, contrary to their work, trace semantics matter for search tasks.
However, we do find that models with unrelated traces are more sample-efficient than SO models on Countdown, which is left unexplained.
This result informs my approach when I describe a proposal to address Gap~2 in Section~\ref{sec:proposal_rq2}.

\paragraph{Knowing the regime yields a more compute-efficient data mixture.}

Our experiments also demonstrate that training a single model on multiple search tasks with an informed choice between SO and SA supervision per task (in our case, SO for maze navigation and SA for modular Countdown) generalises ID with less training compute and comparable dataset sizes (Figure~\ref{fig:final_mixture_error}).

\section{Additional pilot experiments}
\label{sec:progress_pilots}

% NOTE: unpublished pilots; state seeds/settings if any are reported as numbers.
Three further experiments, not included in the paper, inform the research questions of Chapter~\ref{ch:proposal}.
First, pause and filler tokens~\citep{goyalPauseTokens2024,pfauLetsThinkDot2024} appended to SO data are trivially learnable by the model and produce no effect on either task, so the learning problem collapses into SO learning.
Second, models trained with corrupted or unrelated traces on maze navigation learn to ignore the traces once they generalise in-distribution, which likewise collapses to SO learning; only on modular Countdown do unrelated traces retain an effect, as reported above.
Third, the best SA model trained on modular Countdown with three numbers, tested on puzzles with four and five numbers, shows a severe drop in performance; the current trace format and setup therefore do not generalise in length, and this result serves as the control baseline for Section~\ref{sec:proposal_rq3} which addresses Gap~3.

\section{Outlook}
\label{sec:progress_outlook}

Our study reported in this chapter argues that there are search problems on which SA learning is the most sample- and compute-efficient method for in-distribution generalisation, that this is not the case for every search problem, and that which case applies is decided by the problem's structure rather than by model scale.
It defines two learning regimes for search problems that separate the problems by the relative sample and compute efficiency of SA compared to SO learning, and shows that scaling the model size only helps in the regime where SO learning is more efficient.
It also demonstrates that suboptimal or unrelated traces hinder performance and learning efficiency of SA, which may fall behind SO learning even on problems where optimal SA is more efficient.
Finally, it provides the protocol, specifically, matched-compute comparison across data and model sweeps in both regimes, which establishes the regime for a given problem.
For Research Question~1, it leaves open which structural properties determine the regime: the evidence rests on two problems with different structure and representation, and interventions within the problem only show that they do not change the regime; thus, the candidate factors are not yet isolated.
It also leaves open whether other regimes are possible in practice, since the provided definition does not exhaustively define all possible regimes.
For Research Question~2, it only provides evidence that trace quality and semantics matter in the search-efficient regime, but does not explain why that is the case, why suboptimal traces perform worse, and why unrelated traces retain part of the benefit and outperform SO data.
For Research Question~3, it shows only that the current setup does not generalise in length, and everything beyond in-distribution generalisation remains to be done.
Chapter~\ref{ch:proposal} takes up each of these in turn.
